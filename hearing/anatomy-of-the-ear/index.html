Anatomy of the ear

The outer, middle and inner ear, and how they turn sound into signals.

Hearing health

Photo: illustration of the outer, middle and inner ear

Your ear has three parts that work together to turn sound waves in the air into signals your brain understands.

The outer ear: the sound catcher

The outer ear is the part you can see, called the pinna, plus the ear canal. The shape of the pinna funnels sound into the canal and helps you tell where sounds are coming from. The canal carries sound to the eardrum.

Note: ear wax

Ear wax is normal. It protects and cleans the ear canal and usually works its way out on its own. Sometimes it builds up and blocks the canal, which muffles sound. We offer safe wax removal by microsuction.

The middle ear: turning sound into movement

Behind the eardrum is a small, air-filled space that holds three tiny bones: the malleus, incus and stapes. When sound makes the eardrum vibrate, these bones pass the vibration on and amplify it on its way to the inner ear.

Note: Eustachian tubes

The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the back of your throat and keeps the air pressure equal on both sides of the eardrum. When your ears pop on a plane, that's this tube opening.

The inner ear: where sound becomes sensation

The cochlea is a fluid-filled, snail-shaped organ lined with thousands of tiny hair cells. Vibrations move the fluid, the hair cells respond, and the hearing nerve carries the signal to your brain.

Damaged hair cells don't grow back. Their gradual loss with age, called presbycusis, is the most common cause of hearing loss in older adults. That's why regular hearing checks matter as you get older.
